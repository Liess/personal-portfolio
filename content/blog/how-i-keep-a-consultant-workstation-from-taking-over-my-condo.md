# How I Keep a Consultant Workstation From Taking the Whole Condo

Working as a consultant means your workstation can easily become the center of your entire life.

There’s always another ticket to check, another Teams message, another deployment to monitor, another meeting starting in ten minutes. Before you know it, the laptop is on the dining table, cables are running across the floor, monitors are everywhere, and your bedroom starts looking more like a small IT office than a place to actually live.

I’ve been trying to avoid that.

My approach is simple: **the workstation can take up a corner of the room, but it shouldn’t take over the room.**

And in my case, there’s another requirement: **this is also my gaming space.**

## One workstation, two purposes

I don't have a separate office for work and another room for gaming. I use the same workstation for both.

During work hours, it becomes my consultant setup. My production laptop connects to the monitor, keyboard, mouse, and everything else I need to get through the day.

But when I’m done with a task—or just need a short break—I can switch the monitor back to my personal PC with essentially **one click**.

And suddenly, the same workstation becomes my gaming setup.

No moving monitors. No unplugging cables. No rearranging the desk.

Just one click and I can go from looking at a production dashboard to playing a game.

That flexibility is probably one of my favorite things about the setup.

## The production laptop stays separate

One of my biggest rules is keeping the **production/work laptop separate from my personal setup**.

Consulting work often means dealing with client environments, corporate VPNs, security tools, certificates, credentials, and applications that I don't want mixed with my personal computer.

So rather than turning my personal machine into a company-managed device, the work laptop gets its own space.

It can connect to the same monitor and peripherals when I need a larger workspace, but the laptop itself remains clearly identifiable as work equipment.

When the workday is over, I can close it, switch the display back to my PC, and move on.

That physical separation creates a surprisingly effective mental boundary.

## One click from work to gaming

This is where the setup gets especially useful.

The workstation isn't locked into being a "work desk."

The monitor setup allows me to switch between the work laptop and my desktop PC without physically changing the workstation.

**Work → click → gaming.**

It sounds trivial, but it makes a huge difference.

If I want to take a 20-minute break between meetings, I don't need to set anything up. The gaming PC is already there. The keyboard and mouse are already there. The monitor is already there.

And when the break is over:

**Gaming → click → back to work.**

It makes the workstation feel less like a dedicated corporate office and more like a flexible personal command center.

Of course, I try not to turn every "quick game" into a three-hour gaming session during work hours. 😂

## The desk is for work—and play

The biggest advantage is that I don't need additional space for gaming.

The same chair, desk, monitor, speakers, and peripherals serve both purposes.

That's important when you're working with limited condo space.

Instead of having:

**Work desk + gaming desk + office equipment + gaming equipment**

I have:

**One desk that does both.**

The setup can be slightly excessive, but at least the excess is concentrated in one place.

## Cables are the real enemy

The hardest part isn't actually the computers.

It's the cables.

A workstation that supports both work and gaming can accumulate an absurd number of them: laptop chargers, monitor cables, power strips, USB devices, network cables, keyboard and mouse cables, and whatever random adapter you apparently need exactly once every six months.

I try to keep the power and data connections concentrated around the workstation instead of running cables throughout the room.

It isn't perfectly cable-free—and honestly, that's unrealistic with this much equipment—but the goal is that the cables don't visually escape the workstation.

If a cable has no reason to be outside the work zone, it stays inside it.

## I let the setup be a little excessive

There’s also a bit of contradiction here.

The setup is definitely not minimalist.

There are multiple displays, a desktop PC, a laptop, accessories, a mechanical keyboard, figures, and plenty of technology packed into one area.

But that's intentional.

I'd rather have **one concentrated, slightly ridiculous workstation** than have a minimalist setup that slowly spreads into five different parts of the condo.

Concentration is the form of minimalism that works for me.

And because the workstation doubles as my gaming space, I get more value out of every square meter.

## The workstation has a closing time

The most important boundary isn't actually physical.

It's knowing when to stop.

When the workday ends, I close the work laptop. I switch the monitor back to my PC, put away anything that needs to be put away, and leave the chair.

The workstation stays there, ready for tomorrow—but it no longer feels like work.

It becomes my personal space again.

And that's the nice thing about having everything in one place. I don't have to dismantle my office to reclaim my room.

I just switch modes.

## A workstation shouldn't define the room

For me, the goal isn't to have the most impressive home office possible.

It's to have a setup that supports the kind of work I do without making the entire condo feel like an office.

The workstation is concentrated in one corner. The production laptop has its own boundary. The cables stay contained. The equipment has a home.

And with one click, the same space can go from **consultant workstation to gaming setup.**

That's probably the best compromise I've found for condo living.

Because working from home doesn't have to mean **living at work**.

And having a gaming setup doesn't have to mean sacrificing another room for it.

Sometimes, you just need one good workstation that knows how to switch personalities.
